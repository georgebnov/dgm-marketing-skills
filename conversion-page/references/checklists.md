# Conversion page checklists

Read this file at step A2 (audit) or W3 (write). Run the "Every page" section, then the section of the page type. For a `homepage + landing page`, run both sections: the landing page section on the conversion parts, the homepage section on the navigation parts. In write mode, use the same items as the rules for each section that you write.

Each section heading names its tier for step A4. An item marked "(ideally)" is a test idea (tier 7) when it is missing, not a fail.

## Every page

**Hierarchy and layout** (tier 4 for the first screen, tier 6 below it. Tier 3 if the layout hides the primary CTA or the form.)

- [ ] The information hierarchy is clear: the most important thing is the biggest, boldest, and most contrasted. Then the secondary and tertiary things.
- [ ] The key elements are above the fold at phone width.
- [ ] The layout follows a Z or an F pattern. The Z pattern is the common eye path: logo, top-right CTA, diagonally across the hero to the text, then the CTA button. The F pattern is the other common path. (Shopify and other templates already have the Z pattern built in.)
- [ ] The page works on desktop, tablet, half a screen, and phone. A common phone failure: a large logo fills the first screen, so the form does not show.
- [ ] The layout is conventional: content is where people expect it (trust driver).

**Brand** (tier 6)

- [ ] The brand colors are consistent on the page and with the other channels.
- [ ] Small choices are consistent: rounded or straight corners, bold or italics.
- [ ] The design gives the brand feel that the user wants (trustworthy, innovative, professional, fun).

**Hero image or video** (tier 4. A hero that slows the page: tier 2.)

- [ ] High-quality and non-generic: not a stock photo of a person at a laptop. If a stock image is all the user can afford, overlay something that is theirs: a border, a brand element, an icon, or a stamp. This takes about 5 minutes.
- [ ] Inspires action and does not distract. A sad or odd image that has no link to the offer fails.
- [ ] Passes the squint test: it shows what the business does. Ask: could this image belong to a business in a different industry? If yes, it fails.
- [ ] Conveys the tone of the brand (professional, fun, visionary, tech-forward, youthful).
- [ ] (ideally) Contains a person, and the person looks toward the CTA. Eye-direction tests report a conversion uplift when the person in the image looks at the button.
- [ ] Loads fast. A video or GIF does not play behind text and does not slow the page.

**CTA** (tier 3)

- [ ] 1 primary CTA type. Secondary CTAs look secondary (for example a solid green "Buy Now" and an outlined "Learn More").
- [ ] Above the fold, in a sticky CTA, and repeated every few floors. The top right corner is the ideal place for the sticky CTA. A different place is a test idea, not a fail. Check it on a phone. A good pattern: the same "Get a demo" in the nav, at the top, and 2 more times lower.
- [ ] Consistent: 1 verb and 1 color for the primary CTA everywhere. "Get a demo" at the top and "Book a demo" at the bottom is a finding. A green button at the top and a black button at the bottom is a finding.
- [ ] Action-oriented text with a value promise, not boring and not vague. The text says the ask and what comes next.
- [ ] Stands out: contrasting color, larger or bolder text. If you squint, you see it at once.

**Copy** (tier 5, suitability. In the heading or supporting copy: tier 4.)

- [ ] Specific facts, not generic claims. `helpful-content` step 3 (specificity) has the rule and the examples.
- [ ] The problem is in the customer's words.
- [ ] Benefits, not only features.
- [ ] Jargon-free. The copy does not try to sound impressive.

**Trust** (tier 5, trust)

- [ ] Legitimacy signals: a way to reach the business (does it exist, and can a customer reach it?), and recent activity. An address or PO box and a phone number where the business type implies them (for example a local service). For a consumer app, a support email or contact form is enough.
- [ ] The facts agree with each other. 2 different numbers for the same thing (a price, a count, a date) are a fail.
- [ ] E-E-A-T signals: experience in the space, expertise, credentials, star reviews, press, awards.
- [ ] Assurances where the purchase needs them: privacy, payment security, support access, guarantees, returns.

**Technical** (speed: tier 2. Other items: tier 6.)

- [ ] The page loads fast. Run PageSpeed Insights with the mobile setting. Report 2 values: First Contentful Paint (the time until the first text or image shows) and Largest Contentful Paint (the time until the largest visible element shows). Fail at Google's Largest Contentful Paint over 2.5 s. A load under 0.5 s is a stretch target for tests. Without the tool, mark this item "not measured".
- [ ] The URL identifies the page and is nested correctly in the site hierarchy (for example `/collections/skin`, `/collections/best-sellers`).
- [ ] Headers are tagged (H1, H2) for SEO.
- [ ] The page meets accessibility guidelines. This skill names no standard. Report only the concrete problems that you can see: low text contrast, images without alt text, form fields without labels, buttons or links without readable text.

## Homepage

Tier 6, except: no point of conversion is tier 3.

- [ ] It shows what the business offers and does, in the first screen.
- [ ] It shows E-E-A-T signals clearly.
- [ ] It is not a dead end: links send each visitor to the part of the site that they are ready for (Products, Services, Plans, About Us, FAQs, Learn More, Customer Testimonials).
- [ ] It has 1 point of conversion. It is probably not the main click of the site.
- [ ] It does not hold every offer behind anchor links. Each offer has its own page and URL.
- [ ] KPIs to watch: bounce rate, engagement, click-through to the intended pages.

Write-mode sections: brand offer heading, supporting copy, hero, trust signals, links to each main path, 1 conversion point.

## Topic page

Tier 6, except: no CTA (a dead end) is tier 3.

- [ ] It addresses the customer's problems and barriers up front. Model: name the challenge the reader has, then show the offer that solves it.
- [ ] It passes the `helpful-content` rules for answers, specific facts, and structure (steps 2 and 3). Structure for readers. Google says no AI-specific chunking is needed.
- [ ] It offers a middle-step CTA (download research, email sign-up, book a demo) and the full conversion. No dead ends.
- [ ] The conversion button and the main message are at the top. FAQs and extended content are below the main CTA (`helpful-content`, "FAQs"). The visitor does not scroll to find the button.

Write-mode sections: problem heading, answer first, specific facts, middle-step CTA, deep sections (tables, steps, FAQs) below, full conversion CTA.

## Landing page

**Headline** (tier 4)

- [ ] Short, clear, and compelling. Not "The future of ___".
- [ ] Says what we offer, why the visitor should care, and how it relates to their needs.
- [ ] Relates to the copy of the ad that sends traffic. Google Ads scores how well the landing page matches the ad. A bad match (an ad for one product that lands on a page for a different product) costs more per click.

**Supporting copy** (tier 4)

- [ ] Brief, clear, and compelling. A visitor can skim it and get the offer.
- [ ] Adds context with keywords from the ad. It is more than a sales line.

**Form** (tier 3)

- [ ] Only the minimum fields. A 10-field form (full name, phone, email, full street address, product, and "How did you hear about us?") for a simple estimate makes visitors leave at once.
- [ ] It guides the visitor, and says what happens after submit.
- [ ] A long form (about 5 steps or more) is split into parts, and the reward is shown. Long intake forms lose people.

**CTA** (tier 3)

- [ ] Only 1 type of action on the page, repeated several times. Not a middle step such as an email newsletter sign-up.

**Structure** (tier 4 above the fold, tier 6 below it)

- [ ] About 4 or 5 elements above the fold: heading, supporting copy, hero, CTA, nav with sticky CTA.
- [ ] (ideally) Nav stripped or minimized for a paid campaign page, to focus attention on the CTA.
- [ ] Lower floors only if they help. Candidates: key benefits (bullets and icons), features and how it works, expertise and trust credentials (ratings, testimonials, statistics, awards, verifications, press).
- [ ] The page addresses the main pain points, conveys the main benefits, says how it works and what to expect, and shows verification (social proof, trust, credentials).

**Testing** (tier 7)

- [ ] A test plan exists for heading, image, copy, and CTA variants, with a tool such as VWO.
- [ ] The primary metric is the real conversion (for example "Appointment Booked"), not clicks. A small change, such as a review-rating banner, can need a long run and a share of traffic before it reaches significance.
- [ ] Update rhythm: landing pages weekly or biweekly, from data and heatmaps. Do not edit a page while a test runs on it.

## Product page (e-commerce)

Goal: easy to find, easy to buy, relevant. Shoppers come on 2 missions. A focused shopper (knows what they want) wants few clicks and a clear path with top sellers first. A browsing shopper wants inspiration and information. The site must serve both.

**Images** (hero image: tier 4. Carousel and tags: tier 6.) Images are the most important lever on a product page. Online, the shopper cannot touch the product. The image is all they have.

- [ ] Recognize: the image shows at once what the product is and which brand.
- [ ] Entice: it looks attractive. Not a dark or shapeless photo.
- [ ] Clarify: it confirms the details (the 12 pack or the 6 pack, low-fat or full-fat).
- [ ] The hero image passes the thumbnail test on a phone and answers 4 questions (the mobile-ready hero image guidelines from the University of Cambridge and Unilever): who is the brand, what is it, which variety, how much. Example: brand name, "oat milk", "unsweetened", "1 L".
- [ ] A packaged product also shows the product outside the package.
- [ ] Multiple images: composite, marketing, and lifestyle images, every angle, the product in use with people. Video if possible. On Amazon, fewer than 6 high-quality images can get the listing penalized.
- [ ] Images are tagged in the back end, and A/B tested.

**Content** (product name: tier 4. Ratings and reviews: tier 5. Other items: tier 6.)

- [ ] A standard name convention, for example brand, then style, then color. The shopper must not guess what the product is.
- [ ] For search, a complete name: Brand + Product + Format. Better: Brand + Product + Format + Category + Variant, plus color, weight, season, occasion. Some search engines read only the name. Example: "<Brand> Gentle Foaming Face Cleanser for Sensitive Skin, Fragrance-Free, with Ceramides, 473 mL".
- [ ] Metadata holds category keywords, nicknames, typos, and slang.
- [ ] The description is complete (all aspects), compelling, and scannable: bullets, lists, icons, headings. Small packets of facts, not 1 big paragraph (for example a spec table). Expandable sections for the full story (description and fit, materials, care, delivery and payment). Shoppers scan. They do not read long paragraphs.
- [ ] Top questions and FAQs are answered up front (for example "Will it fit in my car?", "Does it come with batteries?"). AI tools split 1 shopper question into many sub-questions, so have the answers on the page.
- [ ] Ratings and reviews are shown (the trust part of E-E-A-T).
- [ ] A brand story: origins, manufacturing, supply chain, if there is something to be proud of.
- [ ] Use the category words that customers and the industry leader use, not internal trade terms or SKU codes.

**Easy to buy** (tier 3)

- [ ] Add to cart is visible and works on every page where the product appears: collections, top sellers, top rated, seasonal pages. The shopper can always click it and always buy.
- [ ] Costs are clear before checkout. Shipping costs that appear late scare shoppers. Checkout is as lean as possible.
- [ ] The service levels are clear: in stock, delivery speed. Stock availability is the most important service level.

**Site checks (when the page is part of a store)** (tier 6)

- [ ] Search the product on the site the way a shopper would. Record failures: a too-simple name, a missing image, an old pack image, an image that does not show the pack size, single-serving items ranked first, or the product not in the results. On-site search drives a large share of basket-adds, many first searches fail, and most shoppers do not look past the first page of results.
- [ ] The best-selling, most profitable product is near the top of its list, not 10th.
- [ ] The nav is intuitive (each product sits in the category where a shopper expects it), directional, educational (visuals for unfamiliar categories, such as tent types or sofa shapes), and inspirational (fresh collections for seasons and trends). Not too much: a menu with too many items at once is a finding.
- [ ] 1 product can live under more than 1 parent category.
- [ ] Filters follow the order of the shopper's decisions. Example for a drink: brand or style first, then pack size, then bottle or can. Do not start with third-level decisions.
- [ ] Breadcrumbs and URL paths use the same customer words as the filters.

Write-mode sections: product name, hero image brief (4 questions), image carousel brief, scannable bullets, specs, FAQs, reviews block, brand story, add to cart.

## CTA cheat sheet

Categories of CTA text: Buy Now Alternatives, Try, Get Started, Enroll/Join/Register, Sign-Up For Something, Download, Create, Send Free Info, Contact Us, General, Demo/Know More.

Active verbs: Start, Stop, Sign-up, Download, Get, Book, Schedule, Buy, Take, Watch, Apply, Build, Learn, Discover, Act now, Enter.

Test variables (change 1 per test):

- The verb: join, click, enroll, or apply.
- First versus third person: "my free trial" versus "your free trial".
- Completeness: "Sign-Up" versus "Sign-Up To Start Saving".
- Urgency: "Try it" versus "Try it Now" versus "Try it Today".
- The button color, shape, and style (only with traffic above the A/B floor in `growth-experiment`).

Examples: "try it for free", "try it now", "book a free demo", "Get a Demo", "Book a Demo", "Request a demo", "Contact Sales", "Add to cart", "Schedule a Meeting", "See it in action", "Speak to our team".

## Worked example: audit of a "Get a free estimate" page

A synthetic example. A home-services company runs a page that offers "$200 off your first install", with the heading "Motorized Window Shades" and a form. In the output format (the number in brackets is the tier from A4):

```
Page: window shades estimate page · type: landing · conversion: estimate request · user group: homeowners (assumed: the user did not name the group)
Verdict: cut the form and bring the CTA and a benefit heading above the fold.

1. [3] Form: non-necessary fields
   evidence: 10 fields (first name, last name, phone, email, street address, city, province, postal code, desired product, "How did you hear about us?")
   fix: keep only the fields the team needs to give an estimate [NEEDS FROM USER: which fields]
2. [3] CTA: below the fold
   evidence: the submit button is not visible in the first screen
   fix: move the form CTA into the first screen, and add a sticky CTA
3. [3] Mobile: logo fills the first screen
   evidence: at phone width the logo takes the room and most of the form does not show
   fix: shrink the logo at phone width so the heading and the form show
4. [4] Heading: names the product, no benefit
   evidence: "Motorized Window Shades"
   fix: write a claim with the benefit, then test about 9 variants
5. [4] Benefits copy: below the fold
   evidence: the benefits copy exists but is not in the first screen
   fix: move 1 benefit line under the heading

Keep: "We will call you within 1 business day with your estimate." It says what happens after submit.
Not measured: load time (the audit used screenshots only)
Tests to run: strip the nav (each link is an exit) · a hero with a person who looks toward the form · about 9 heading variants · each judged on estimate requests
Next review: weekly or biweekly (landing page)
```
