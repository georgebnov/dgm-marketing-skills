# Pricing checks

Read this file at step 2 when the material has a price, a sale, "was", "regular", "compare at", a fee, or a checkout. Every check here is High unless it says otherwise.

Pricing is the top enforcement priority of the Competition Bureau. There are many federal consent agreements for millions of dollars. A consent agreement is a settlement that a business signs with the Bureau. For a price, materiality is easy to show and the damages are easy to quantify. Price is always material. A hidden fee is easy to quantify: the fee times the number of transactions. This claim is easy to make out for a consumer class action and for an enforcement agency. Watch it closely if the business is very promotional.

## Drip pricing (High)

**Definition.** Drip pricing is an advertised price that does not include mandatory fees or other costs that are added later. Example: the first-touch ad says $10, and a $1.50 fee appears at checkout.

**Case.** Cineplex charged a $1.50 online booking fee. The Competition Tribunal ruled against Cineplex in 2024 and ordered a penalty of about $38.9 million. Cineplex appealed the decision. The damages were the $1.50 fee times the number of transactions in the investigation period.

**Check.**

1. Find the first price representation: the first price that the consumer sees (the ad, the product tile, the landing page).
2. Follow the checkout flow to the final total. List every fee that appears on the way.
3. For each fee, decide which kind it is:
   - Taxes and other government charges imposed on the purchaser (a consumer or a business). These can come later.
   - Fees that are not fixed or not known in advance. Example: shipping that depends on where in Canada the buyer lives. Shipping to Whitehorse costs a different amount than shipping to Toronto. These can come later.
   - Every other non-optional fixed fee. It must be in the first price.
4. Result: pass if every non-optional fixed fee is in the first price. Fail if 1 or more is not. Not checked if you cannot see the checkout.

**Fix.** Put the fee in the first price, or remove the fee.

## "Regular" price claims: "Now $10 / Was $20", "Our price $10 / Compare at $20" (High)

You must substantiate the $20. There are 2 legal tests. The time test is the one that most businesses use. The other test is less popular and harder to meet. This skill covers only the time test. If the user relies on the other test, put it in "Not covered: lawyer".

The test has 2 components. Both must pass.

**1. Time.**

- Look at the 6 months before the sale starts.
- On at least 51% of the days in that period, the business must have offered the product or service at the regular price or a higher price.
- Coupons and one-to-one deals are not prices to the public. Leave them out of the calculation.
- **Rolling test.** Do the calculation again for each day of the sale. For a week-long sale, look back 6 months from day 1, then from day 2, and so on to the last day. Every day must pass.

**2. Good faith.** During that period:

- The product was actually available to buy, in a store or online, with enough supply.
- The regular price was a price that the business expected the market to accept. An absurd regular price (a pen at $1 million) fails: nobody would buy at that price. Support it with price audits or a competitive shop that show the pricing philosophy and the market position of the business.
- The business had some sales volume at the regular price. There is no legal threshold. In one case, about 10% was not enough volume.

**"Compare at" (market comparisons).** The same test applies. Use the regular price in the market for the last 6 months, not the business's own price. This is harder, because you must find the regular prices of competitors.

**The reference period can change.**

- Seasonal products (Christmas lights, apparel) can use a shorter or longer period.
- For refrigerators, you may want an annual period. A longer period stops one sale event (for example Black Friday on TVs) from blocking the regular price.
- If the user uses a period other than 6 months, record the period and the reason in the finding.

**Results.**

- pass: the daily price history and the good faith records exist and meet both components for every day of the sale.
- partial: the time component passes, but the good faith records are missing (or the reverse).
- not checked: no price history was supplied. Name the proof: "6 months of daily price history for <product>, at <regular price> or higher on at least 51% of days, for each day of the sale, plus availability records, a price audit or competitive shop, and sales volume at the regular price."
- fail: the history shows fewer than 51% of days at the regular price or higher on any day of the sale.

**Fix.** Remove the "was", "regular", or "compare at" price until the proof exists. Or change the reference price to one that passes.

## Other price claims

Any other price claim ("this is a sale", "there's a discount", "save X") is a claim. Step 1 of SKILL.md applies: the general impression of the claim needs proof that exists before publishing.

## "Free" claims, planned prices, and paid add-ons

The source gave no specific rule for "free" claims or for a future price. Use the general rules:

1. Run step 1 of SKILL.md on the claim. Find the general impression of "free", "free forever", "free during beta", or "free to join". A "free" claim that drives a sign-up is material.
2. A planned price, a time limit, or a paid add-on is a critical condition. It goes up front in clear, basic words, not in long terms and conditions (the fine print rule of SKILL.md step 1). Level if it is buried: Medium.
3. A paid add-on that is a non-optional fixed fee at purchase is drip pricing (see above). An optional add-on is not part of the drip pricing rule.
4. A "stays free" or "free forever" promise is a claim about the future. The source did not say what proof it needs. **(skill choice)** Give it "Not covered: lawyer", because it is a material price claim.

## AI and prices (High)

If AI makes or changes price claims, a human must review each one before publishing. AI at scale with no review is a large business risk. Price claims are a clear example.
