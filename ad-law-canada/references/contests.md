# Contest checks

Read this file at step 2 when the material has a prize, a giveaway, a draw, a sweepstakes, or "chance to win".

## Is it a contest?

A contest distributes prizes on the basis of chance. The chance can be in the selection of winners or in the distribution of prizes.

- Example of a contest: you buy something, and some buyers get a $5 prize and some get a $5,000 prize.
- A sweepstakes, such as a draw, is also a contest.
- A gift with purchase is not a contest. Everybody who buys gets the gift, so there is no chance. This is true even if the quantity is limited. If the promotion is a gift with purchase, stop this file. Step 1 of SKILL.md still applies to the offer, and the critical terms (for example a limited quantity) go up front.

## Why it is serious

The rules come from the Criminal Code. Their purpose is to stop a contest from becoming an illegal lottery. A lottery must be registered with the gaming authority of the province. A commercial business will not get that authorization. Only government lotteries and charities can get lottery registrations. So a contest that does not comply can be a criminal offence.

## The 4 key requirements

### 1. Test of skill (High)

If the contest has an element of chance, it must also have a component of skill.

- A pure skill contest is unusual. An example is a photography competition where judges assess all the entries. A pure skill contest passes this check if the judging criteria decide the winner.
- The common way to comply is a math question with several operations (for example 4 steps of addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division). It is a safe choice.
- Only the winner must complete the test. It can happen after the selection. If the winner cannot answer, the business must disqualify the winner.
- The business can ask all entrants instead. Then it never has a winner who was drawn and then gets the question wrong.

Result: pass if the rules state a skill-testing question and a disqualification on a wrong answer. Partial if the question exists but the disqualification rule does not. Fail if there is no skill component.

### 2. No forced purchase (High)

The business cannot force a purchase to enter. A purchase can be one way to enter, if the business also gives an alternative method of entry. A classic example is to email in an entry with an essay.

Result: pass if a no-purchase entry method exists and the advertising says so. Fail if a purchase is the only way to enter.

### 3. Mini rules in all contest advertising (Medium)

The Competition Act requires basic details in all contest advertising. If the material says "chance to win", the mini rules go there. Check each piece of contest advertising (each ad, post, email, and page) for:

- the odds of winning,
- the number and value of the prizes,
- the key dates (for example, entry dates) and the eligibility,
- the fact that there is a skill-testing question,
- a link to the long rules, if the business uses them.

Result: pass if all items are present. Partial if some are present (name the missing ones). Fail if none are present.

**Long rules (Low).** The law does not require long rules. They are recommended, because they protect the contest sponsor and give more detail, for example limitations of liability. A missing set of long rules is Low.

### 4. Quebec (Medium)

The only extra requirement for Quebec is French, which general advertising law already requires. Quebec used to require registration of contests with a government authority. That requirement no longer applies. If the user plans to register the contest in Quebec because of the old rule, say that the requirement no longer applies.
