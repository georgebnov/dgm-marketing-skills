# Helpful Content

Google ranks the page that best satisfies the query intent, from a source that people can trust. This skill turns Google's two primary documents into a write and review workflow. The full question lists and source facts are in [REFERENCE.md](REFERENCE.md). A line marked "Practice" is a rule from notes on the TMU Digital Growth Marketing program (SEO and site structure sessions), not from Google. If a practice rule and a Google statement disagree, the Google statement wins.

## Terms

- **E-E-A-T**: Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trustworthiness. Google systems look for signals of these 4 aspects. Trust is the most important. A page does not need all 4.
- **YMYL** ("Your Money or Your Life"): a topic that can affect health, money, safety, or the welfare of society. Google gives E-E-A-T more weight here. The content must match expert consensus.
- **Main content**: each part of the page that directly helps the page achieve its purpose. This includes text, media, tools, useful user comments, tabbed sections, the title, and the headings.
- **Search-engine-first content**: content made primarily to get search visits, not to help a reader.

## Precedence

This skill wins over any other SEO skill on these points. Google states each one.

- Google has no preferred word count. Never set a word-count target. Set a coverage target: what the reader must learn to reach the goal.
- Do not change a page date unless the content changed substantially.
- Do not add or delete pages only to make the site look fresh.
- Quality raters do not control ranking. Use their guidelines only for self-assessment.

These points also apply when another installed SEO skill gives refresh advice or a minimum word count.

This skill owns content and E-E-A-T. If another SEO skill is installed, let it own metadata, schema, and images.

## Workflow

Do the steps in order. Each step needs evidence from the page text. Copy this checklist into the reply.

```
- [ ] 1. Purpose and intent
- [ ] 2. Relevance
- [ ] 3. Main content quality
- [ ] 4. E-E-A-T and Who/How/Why
- [ ] 5. Usability
- [ ] 6. Warning signs
```

1. **Purpose and intent.** Write the target query and the page purpose in 1 line each. Search the query. Note the page types in the top results (guide, tool, product page, local listing). Note if the query needs fresh content (news, scores, prices). If the page type does not match the results, report it first. A mismatch blocks the other steps.
   - Practice, the results-page inventory:
     1. Open an incognito window. Type the query in 2 or 3 plain words, as a customer types it before they know the business.
     2. Note the ads, the organic results, the videos, the news, and the images. Note if a Google Business Profile panel shows. This panel is Google's free business listing (formerly Google My Business), with the address, hours, photos, and review stars.
     3. Copy the "People also ask" and "People also search for" lists. Each item is a candidate subtopic or FAQ.
     4. Read the competitor titles and descriptions. Note each trust proof in a title, for example a review count: "Emergency Plumber in Hamilton | 900+ 5-Star Reviews".
     5. Open the top sites and note their page lists and structure. Do not copy them. Find the gap.
   - Practice, the 3 query types. Write the page's queries in each type:
     - Short tail: 1 to 3 words, high volume, unclear intent. Example: "bookkeeper".
     - Long tail: more specific words, lower volume, higher intent. Example: "bookkeeper for restaurants Hamilton". A place name, "best", and price words ("affordable", "cost") raise intent.
     - AI-engine phrases: full sentences with context. Example: "I run a small restaurant in Hamilton. What does a bookkeeper cost per month, and do I need one for HST?"
2. **Relevance.** Check that the query words, or close synonyms, appear in the title, the main heading, and the body. Check that the page covers related subtopics beyond the keyword: examples, images, lists, steps. Never repeat the keyword to raise relevance.
   - Practice: use exactly 1 H1 (the main heading tag), with the page's primary query in it.
   - Practice: state what the page gives in the first 150 words. The first paragraph is the strongest signal of the topic and the fit to the intent. Google does not state this rule.
   - Practice: lead with the answer. Put the direct answer to the customer's question at the top of its section. Google sets no word position for the answer. Word positions in other SEO tools (first 100 words, first 40-60 words) are third-party heuristics.
   - Practice: write for meaning, not for keywords. AI tools read the semantic meaning. Keyword stuffing no longer works.
   - Practice: give each service, offer, or user group its own page and its own URL. Do not put all offers on the homepage behind anchor links. Example: a bookkeeping firm gets a full page for each service (payroll, HST filing, year-end), with the process, the price range, and the staff credentials for that service.
3. **Main content quality.** Score 4 attributes as strong, weak, or missing. Use this scale also for E-E-A-T in the report. Do not convert it to a numeric score.
   - Effort: human work that went into the page. Generated text with no human review scores missing. A citation does not replace effort.
   - Originality: information or a view that other sites do not have.
   - Talent or skill: clear writing, good media, tools that work.
   - Accuracy: facts that a reader can check. On a YMYL topic, require expert consensus.
   - Practice, specificity: replace each vague claim with a number or a fact that only this business can state. Write "our clients close their books in 4 days on average, from 11 days before", not "faster". Write "clients save up to 15% on fees", not "we help you save money". Facts that only this business can state are the content that AI tools extract and cite.
   - Practice: a page that is only AI output does not rank on any search engine. More low-quality pages do not help.
   - Practice, structure for readers: tagged headers (H1, H2), short paragraphs, tables (for example a comparison table), step-by-step guides, and bulleted lists. Google says no AI-specific chunking is needed (REFERENCE.md, source C).
4. **E-E-A-T and Who/How/Why.**
   - Who: a byline where a reader expects one. The byline links to an author page with real background. Never invent an author, a headshot, or a credential. Google treats this as deception.
   - How: show the method. For a review, state what you tested, how, and the results, with photos as proof. If automation wrote a substantial part, disclose it and say why.
   - Why: the page must help a reader who arrives directly, not only from search.
   - Experience: first-hand details (used the product, visited the place, did the job).
   - Trust: sources cited, an About page, contact information, no factual errors.
   - Practice, proof for each aspect:
     - Experience: specific personal examples with numbers, dates, and locations, case studies, and photos of the work. Example: "in March 2025 we rebuilt the books of a 3-location café after a failed software migration."
     - Expertise: knowledge deeper than the surface, credible sources, and the credentials behind the skill (a degree, a certification, a professional profile).
     - Authoritativeness: published work, speaking engagements, media appearances, and client testimonials. Mentions on other sites are a large part of this aspect. A business that no other site mentions is weak here.
     - Trust: accurate contact information, a publication or update date, and links to credible sources (.gov, .edu, established organizations).
   - Practice: the homepage carries the site's E-E-A-T credentials. It shows experience, expertise, reputation, credentials, and star reviews clearly, to the user and to Google.
5. **Usability.** Check that the page works on a phone and loads fast. Report a page experience problem, but rank it below steps 1 to 4. Google uses usability mainly when other signals are equal.
   - Practice, the technical items for each page:
     - A title tag and a meta description, set in the page settings. The title is the blue headline in the results. The description is the text under it.
     - Internal links to related pages on the site, and external links to credible sources.
     - Speed: test with Google PageSpeed Insights (https://pagespeed.web.dev/). Phone: check with the Lighthouse panel in Chrome DevTools.
     - A descriptive URL: `/payroll-services`, not `/xyz123`.
     - Readability: white space, short sentences, subheadings, and bullets.
     - Alt text that describes each image: "bookkeeper reviewing receipts with a café owner at the counter", not "logo".
   - Practice: nest each URL where the page sits in the site hierarchy, so that people, AI, and search engines can follow it. Example: `/services/payroll` and `/services/hst-filing`.
6. **Warning signs.** Run the search-engine-first list in [REFERENCE.md](REFERENCE.md). Each "yes" is a finding.
   - Practice: then run the checks in "FAQs and off-page signals" below. Report each gap as a finding.

## FAQs and off-page signals (practice rules)

**FAQs.** FAQs feed both traditional search and AI answer engines.

- Put FAQs on every page. Exception: a paid-campaign landing page (`conversion-page` type "landing page"). There, add an FAQ only if it answers an objection to the conversion. Example: a page for restaurant bookkeeping has about 10 FAQs, such as "How much does a restaurant bookkeeper cost?" and "Can you fix last year's books before tax season?"
- Take the questions from real sources: the "People also ask" list, the AI-engine phrases from step 1, and the questions that customers and the sales team hear. Ask the sales team which questions they hear most often.
- Fan out each core question. A fan-out is every version of the question that people ask about 1 core keyword ("books are behind", "how do I catch up on bookkeeping"). answerthepublic.com lists them. Start with the top 5 to 10 customer concerns.
- Write each answer in the customer's words. Answer each question as it comes up. A list of keyword variants is keyword stuffing, not an FAQ.
- Extra content helps AI tools extract and cite the page. This is true only if each part legitimately answers a real question and is unique to the business. Put the FAQs and the extended content below the main message: in collapsible sections, behind a table of contents, or below the fold. This rule does not set a word count (see "Precedence").

**Backlinks.** A backlink is a link from another site to this site. Google's ranking document also counts links and references from prominent sites as a quality signal.

- Get links from credible sites: industry publications, media outlets, and directories.
- Find unlinked mentions: a podcast feature, a guest blog post, or a quote that names the business with no link. Ask for the link.
- A link that is paid, exchanged for value, or in a guest post that the business wrote must carry `rel="sponsored"` or `rel="nofollow"`. Google's link spam policy states this rule. Source: https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies#link-spam
- Create a Google Alert for the brand name. The alert reports new web content that names the brand.

**Local search.** If step 1 shows a Google Business Profile panel, the business needs a complete, verified profile. Google uses the profile to check the location, the legitimacy, the consistency with the website, and the local relevance ("plumbing company near me").

- Complete all sections: hours, phone, address, website, and categories.
- Add good photos of the storefront, products, services, and team. Do not upload dark photos.
- Collect reviews and respond to them. Reviews remain a strong local signal.
- Post regular updates (news, offers, events).

**Measurement.** When the page is live, read Google Search Console each month. Search Console is Google's free tool that shows how the site does in Google Search. Read the clicks, the impressions, the CTR (clicks divided by impressions), and the average position. Read the exact queries that the page ranks for. Compare the queries with the target queries from step 1. Plan to move specific positions up, for example from number 4 to number 1.

## Output

Give the verdict first in 1 line: "ready", "fix first", or "rewrite". Then list each finding, most severe first:

```
[step] <problem> | evidence: "<quote from the page>" | fix: <one concrete action>
```

A fix names the exact content to add. Write "add the test setup: 3 laptops, 40 hours of battery tests, photos of each". Do not write "add more expertise".

Tag a finding from a practice rule as `[step, practice]`, so that the reader can tell it from a Google rule.

## Writing mode

When you write a page, not review it, do step 1 first. Ask the user for the first-hand material that only they have: numbers, dates, photos, test results, client cases. Never invent this material. If they have none, say that the page will score weak on Experience. Then write, and run steps 2 to 6 on the draft.

Practice: in step 1, do the results-page inventory and write the 3 query types before the first draft. Use the "People also ask" items and the AI-engine phrases as the FAQ list. Do not copy competitor titles word for word.
