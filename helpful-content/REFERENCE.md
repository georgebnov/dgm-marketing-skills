## How ranking works (source B, paraphrased)

Google weighs 5 groups of signals. The weight of each group changes with the query. For news, freshness has more weight than for a dictionary definition.

1. **Meaning.** Language models find the intent of the query. Spelling correction and synonyms connect "change laptop brightness" to a page that says "adjust laptop brightness". Words like "recipe" or "pictures", the query language, local intent ("pizza"), and current events change which results fit.
2. **Relevance.** The basic signal is the query keywords in the headings or the body. Google also uses aggregated, anonymized interaction data. A page that repeats "dogs" many times is not more relevant. A page with dog photos, videos, and a list of breeds is more relevant.
3. **Quality.** After relevance, Google prefers content with signals of expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness. One factor: other prominent sites link to or refer to the content. Rater feedback helps Google tune the systems.
4. **Usability.** When other signals are about equal, the more accessible page can rank higher. Example: a page that works on a phone and loads fast.
5. **Context.** Location, search history, and settings change results. "Football" in Chicago and in London give different results.

Source B uses "expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness" in one paragraph and adds "experience" in another. Source A uses E-E-A-T. This skill uses E-E-A-T.

## Content and quality questions (source A)

- Does the content give original information, reporting, research, or analysis?
- Does it describe the topic substantially, completely, or comprehensively?
- Does it give insight beyond the obvious?
- If it uses other sources, does it add substantial value, not only copy or rewrite them?
- Does the main heading or title summarize the content accurately?
- Does the heading or title avoid exaggeration and shock?
- Would a reader bookmark it, share it, or recommend it?
- Would a printed magazine, encyclopedia, or book reference it?
- Does it give substantial value compared with other pages in the results?
- Does it have spelling or style errors?
- Is it produced well, or does it look hasty?
- Is it mass-produced, outsourced to many creators, or spread across many sites, so that each page gets little care?

## Expertise questions (source A)

- Does the page make a reader trust it: clear sources, proof of expertise, author or site background (author page, About page)?
- Would a person who researches the site find that it is well trusted or recognized as an authority on the topic?
- Did an expert or an enthusiast who knows the topic well write or review it?
- Does it have factual errors that are easy to check?

## People-first questions (source A)

A "yes" is a good sign.

- Does the site have an existing or intended audience that would find the content useful if they came directly?
- Does the content show first-hand expertise and depth of knowledge (used the product, visited the place)?
- Does the site have a primary purpose or focus?
- After reading, does the reader know enough to reach the goal?
- Will the reader feel satisfied?

## Search-engine-first warning signs (source A)

A "yes" is a warning sign.

- Is the content primarily made to attract search visits?
- Do you produce much content on many topics in the hope that some ranks?
- Do you use much automation to produce content on many topics?
- Do you mainly summarize others and add little value?
- Do you write about trends only because they trend, not for your audience?
- Do readers need to search again to get better information?
- Do you write to a word count because you think Google prefers one? (Google has no preferred word count.)
- Did you enter a niche without real expertise, only for search traffic?
- Does the content promise an answer that does not exist, for example an unconfirmed release date?
- Do you change page dates to look fresh when the content did not change substantially?
- Do you add or remove much content mainly to make the site look fresh? (It does not help.)

## E-E-A-T and raters (source A)

- Trust is the most important aspect. Experience, expertise, and authoritativeness contribute to trust.
- Content can be helpful from experience alone, or from expertise alone.
- YMYL topics get more weight on E-E-A-T.
- Search quality raters are people who check if results seem good. They have no control over ranking. Google does not use rater data directly in ranking algorithms.
- The Search Quality Evaluator Guidelines say that main content quality is one of the most important factors.

## Main content attributes (source A)

- **Effort**: human work in the content or in the system behind it. Original analysis, manual translation, or a custom map is high effort. Pages generated from feeds, or much AI text with no human review, are low effort. Attribution does not replace effort.
- **Originality**: information or views that other sites do not have.
- **Talent or skill**: clear writing, good video, tools that work. Not all content needs special expertise. A personal snow-clearing tip does not.
- **Accuracy**: informational pages must be factually correct. YMYL pages must be highly accurate and match expert consensus.

## Who, How, Why (source A)

- **Who**: Is the author clear? Is there a byline where a reader expects one? Does it link to background on the author? A fake author profile (AI headshot, invented name, false credentials) is deception. Deception makes the page untrustworthy and is a low-quality signal.
- **How**: Show the process. For product reviews: the number of products tested, the results, the test method, and proof such as photos. If automation substantially generated the content: Is that clear to visitors? Do you explain how and why you used it? Add a disclosure where a reader would ask "How was this created?"
- **Why**: The most important question. The reason must be to help people. Automation used primarily to manipulate ranking violates Google's spam policies.

## SEO and people-first content (source A)

SEO helps search engines find and understand content. Google says SEO is helpful when you apply it to people-first content.
